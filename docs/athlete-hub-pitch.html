Where the hub should live

Two defensible paths. The trade is speed versus brand equity — a business call, not a technical one.

Option A

wasabirub.com

sportpharm.com stays the company site. The hub and the product live together, so everything the content earns compounds into the WasabiRub brand.

Gain: traffic lands where the purchase happens, and every ranking, backlink and share builds the product brand itself.

Cost: a new domain starts at zero search authority. Expect 6–12 months to meaningful organic traffic.

Option B

sportpharm.com/athlete-hub

The hub sits on the established domain and drives hard to wasabirub.com from every page.

Gain: inherits years of existing domain authority — the single biggest accelerant available. Ranks materially sooner.

Cost: traffic lands on SportPharm first, adding a step before purchase, and the equity builds under SportPharm rather than WasabiRub.

The real question is not which ranks faster — it is what we are building. Option A trades speed for brand equity in WasabiRub. Option B trades brand concentration for reach sooner. Both are defensible; they answer different questions.

Worth saying plainly

Health content is held to a higher standard by search engines than almost any other category, and we would be building from zero on a domain that sells the product. Anyone promising fast rankings here is guessing. This is a compounding investment, not a campaign — but unlike advertising, it does not stop the day we stop paying.

Two stores, same three products

The hub, the products and the cart all live on wasabirub.com — so nothing is lost in transit. The problem is on the other side of the split.

sportpharm.com is WordPress running WooCommerce. It sells WasabiRub, IcetraRub and Super Hot today. wasabirub.com will sell the same three products through Payload + Stripe. Same catalogue, two systems that do not know about each other.

This is the real version of the concern raised in the room: not a cart that empties in transit, but two carts that never see each other. A customer who starts on sportpharm.com and finishes on wasabirub.com has a half-filled basket on a site they have left — and we would be reconciling two order streams, two stock counts and two discount systems for one product line.

Recommendation: wasabirub.com becomes the only store. sportpharm.com stops selling and becomes what it is best at — the company, the pharmacy, the brick-and-mortar presence — with a Store item in its menu that deep-links to wasabirub.com. One catalogue, one checkout, one order stream, one place a customer's history lives.

That is already the direction in the site notes. It needs saying out loud on Tuesday, because it means retiring WooCommerce as a selling system — a decision with an owner and a migration attached, not a toggle.

What “rebuilding commerce ops” actually costs

Less than the WordPress column implies. For a three-SKU catalogue most of it is configuration of managed services, not a build.

TaxStripe Tax — one flag on the checkout session. Rate lookup, US economic-nexus monitoring and filing. A tax code per product; no tables to maintain
ShippingFlat or tiered rates on the checkout session, or Shippo for live carrier rates and labels — which we already run on another product
RefundsStripe Dashboard, no code
InventoryThree SKUs. A quantity column and a decrement on order
Order adminA Payload collection plus the Stripe Dashboard

WooCommerce's inventory and shipping machinery earns its keep on a 500-SKU catalogue. We have three, going on four with LidoRub. One item does deserve care: OTC topicals are not uniformly taxable — some states exempt OTC drugs and others do not. That is precisely what Stripe's product tax codes exist for.

The trap

“Integrate WooCommerce into the new site” means running WordPress headless — alive purely as a commerce backend, talking to the hub over the Store API with cart-token session handling that is notoriously fiddly. We would maintain both stacks plus the glue between them, and payments would still settle on Stripe underneath. Most work, least benefit.

Recommendation: the custom stack. The hub's value is the interactive tools and the employee review workflow — the two things WordPress fights hardest. That was the reasoning behind the original platform decision, and it has not changed.

We have already built this once

Tiny Clothing Swap runs the exact pattern:

  1. The cart lives in the app, not in Stripe — guest carts hold item IDs in local storage, signed-in carts come from the database
  2. At checkout a server function builds the Stripe line items, recomputing every price server-side so a tampered cart cannot underpay, and adds shipping as its own line
  3. Stripe sees a finished basket once, at the moment of payment
  4. A webhook writes the order and its line items back to our database

WasabiRub is the simpler case: three products, one seller, no marketplace payouts and no per-seller shipping split. A known quantity, not a research project.

Who runs the store day to day?

The usual argument for buying a commerce platform is that non-technical staff need to manage it without a developer. Worth being precise, because we are closer to that than it first appears.

From Stripe directly, a non-technical person can already create promotion codes with limits and expiry, issue full or partial refunds, and see every payment and customer. Real, and no-code.

What Stripe cannot be is the product catalogue or the order desk. Stripe has payments, not orders — no fulfilment status, tracking number or shipped/delivered state. And a product in Stripe is a price, not a page: images, description, actives and dosage live in our CMS regardless.

The dashboard is not something we would build — Payload is that dashboard. Defining Products, Orders and Promotions as collections generates the admin UI, with roles so a marketing coordinator sees merchandising and not the company's finances. Same mechanism already carrying the article draft → review → publish workflow.

So the honest gap between us and Shopify is not basic product, order and promo management. It is the long tail: abandoned-cart flows, discount-stacking rules, gift cards, multi-channel selling and built-in commerce analytics. Real features — but a different argument from “our team could not operate it.”

If we chose Shopify later, is this work wasted?

Almost none of it — and this is the cheapest moment to decide.

Unaffected

Effectively the whole build

Every article, all four pathways, both interactive tools, the care guidance, the design system and the CMS review workflow. Content and UX — independent of who processes the payment.

Tied to the choice

The checkout layer only

Cart UI, the session-building function, and add-to-cart wiring on product pages. Today none of it exists — the prototype went away with the layout revert, and there is no payment code on the site.

The cost of switching is near zero right now and grows once checkout is built and real customers and orders live in it. An argument for deciding deliberately on Tuesday, not for deferring. Worth noting too: Shopify headless would not displace Payload for content — commerce platform for commerce, headless CMS for content is a common pairing, so this is not an all-or-nothing fork.

The migration question

Either path has to answer it: existing WooCommerce customers and order history. Migrate them, or make a clean break and keep WooCommerce read-only for historical lookups. Cheap to decide now, expensive to decide after the new store takes its first order.

One technical caution

Stripe payment links and a cart are not the same thing. Payment Links check out one product at a time and cannot do a multi-item basket, order-level promo codes, or free-shipping-over-$75 — all of which the prototype cart had. A real cart means Stripe Checkout with line items assembled by the Payload backend. Well-trodden, and the chosen stack supports it — but back-end work, not a link pasted onto a product page. Worth being precise about before it is scoped as the easy part.

Status today

None of it is wired. There is no Stripe code on the live site and no working cart — the prototype went away with the WasabiRub layout revert. Every Add to Cart button currently on the site shows a toast and does nothing.

Abandoned cart, specifically

Leadership cares about this one, so it is worth being exact.

WooCommerceNot a core feature. It does not exist without a plugin (Tyche, FunnelKit, CartFlows) or an ESP integration. Worth establishing which plugin runs on sportpharm.com today and whether anyone maintains it — “we have abandoned cart” often means a plugin nobody has updated in two years
ShopifyNative. Abandoned checkouts are a first-class object in admin with built-in recovery emails and configurable timing. Works on day one
Custom stackGenuinely buildable, and already in the plan (Payload + Stripe + Mailchimp). Because the cart lives in our own database rather than a plugin's, we get better data — exact contents, exact staleness, our own trigger rules. A scheduled job and an email template, not a platform

The constraint all three share

Recovery needs an email address. Anyone who fills a cart and leaves before entering contact details is invisible to every system. WooCommerce plugins capture it as the customer types at checkout; Shopify captures at the contact step. Nobody recovers a truly anonymous cart.

On prescriptions, since it will come up

Shopify prohibits prescription fulfilment. That looks like a dealbreaker for a pharmacy and mostly is not: Rx does not belong in a retail cart under any of these options. Dispensing runs through a pharmacy management system; what sits on a website is a refill or transfer request that hands off to it.

WooCommerce imposes no such restriction — it is self-hosted software with no acceptable-use policy — but the gates simply move: the payment processor (pharmaceuticals are restricted by Stripe, PayPal and Square alike), state-by-state nonresident pharmacy licensure, DEA registration for controlled substances, and HIPAA obligations once prescription data is in scope.

Where it would genuinely matter

If we wanted refill requests and OTC purchases inside one logged-in account experience. That is a real product idea, and the one scenario where Shopify's policy is a hard constraint. If it is on anyone's roadmap, say so on Tuesday — it changes the answer. Specifics should be confirmed with compliance rather than taken from this document.

The recommendation

Contingent on the answer above — and reversible if that answer is “small.”

Recommendation

If retail POS is in scope: one Shopify store as the commerce backend, the hub keeping its own frontend against the Storefront API, checkout on wasabirub.com, sportpharm.com stops selling and links to it.

If POS is not in scope: keep WordPress and WooCommerce and move the hub onto it. One system, no migration, editorial workflow free. The choice genuinely turns on the POS answer — see the next section.

Content, articles and the interactive tools never touch Shopify. It handles catalogue, cart, checkout, tax, shipping, inventory, refunds and abandoned cart. This is not the headless-WooCommerce trap — Shopify's Storefront API is built for exactly this, where WooCommerce's is a bolt-on.

The honest cost

Subscription, transaction fees if we do not use Shopify Payments, and app subscriptions — real money that scales with success. Less control over checkout UX on standard plans. And a migration: product data, customer accounts, order history, product-URL SEO and retraining. None of that is trivial and none of it should be waved away.

When this recommendation reverses

If retail POS is not in scope, sportpharm.com is never moving, and we stay at three or four products — use Stripe. Do not buy a platform for that. The recommendation follows from the store-size answer, not the other way round.

A useful side effect: this frees the domain decision. The cart-crossing concern disappears with one backend and one checkout, so the choice returns to what it should be — brand equity versus search authority. On that basis, wasabirub.com.

“Why wouldn't we just stay on WordPress?”

The most reasonable question in the room — and the answer is more favourable to WordPress than a first pass suggests.

A correction worth making out loud

WordPress can host everything we have built. We audited all 63 pages: no server-side code, no build step, no API layer, and exactly one external call in the entire site — the contact form. The interactive tools are 236KB of client-side JavaScript that would run identically inside a WordPress page template.

Three claims that do not survive checking: “we would lose the interactive tools” — false, they are pure client-side JavaScript. “We would lose the review workflow” — false and backwards; draft → review → publish with roles is what WordPress was built for, and building it ourselves reimplements what it gives free. “The hub is a Next.js application” — not yet; it is 63 static HTML files, so moving it is porting templates, not rewriting an app. Anyone in the room who knows WordPress would have caught those.

The design does not change — no page builder is involved

The most common and most reasonable fear: we would not rebuild the site out of WordPress components. A custom theme template outputs whatever HTML we give it — our markup goes in and renders exactly as written, same CSS, same typography, same brand red. Elementor, Divi, WPBakery and off-the-shelf themes are where “dated and tacky” comes from, and none are required or involved.

Where an editing interface appears is articles, which is already how ours work: the template owns the layout, the editor supplies title, category and body. Landing pages stay developer-only templates. Custom fields hold structured content that our own markup renders — no visual builder anywhere.

The one real hazard is drift — a plugin installed later that injects its own CSS can degrade a carefully built design. A governance rule, not a technical limit, but it needs an owner. This cuts the same way on Shopify, where Liquid templates also render our own HTML: under every option on the table, the design work survives intact.

What this unlocks for Tuesday

It lets us separate two decisions that have been travelling together.

Framed as “move to a new platform,” this asks leadership to approve a rebuild of the company's website and store at once. Framed accurately — “add a content hub to the site we already run” — it is an addition to a system they are already comfortable with. The store does not move; WooCommerce keeps its orders, customers and plugins untouched.

That means we can ship the hub now and decide the commerce platform later, on its own merits, rather than making the content strategy hostage to a platform migration.

What this does not avoid is the design conversation. Putting a new visual system onto sportpharm.com is precisely the change people are nervous about, and porting into WordPress does not soften that — it only removes the platform argument from it. Two separate objections; they should be answered separately.

What it actually requires

Ongoing, not one-time. This is the part that decides whether it works.

  • Clinical review of every article. Pharmacist and sports-medicine sign-off, with named authors and reviewers published on the page. This is an ethical obligation and a ranking requirement — search engines explicitly weight author credentials for health content.
  • A publishing cadence. A hub that stops updating stops ranking. This needs an owner, not a launch date.
  • Editorial discipline. The moment articles become disguised product pitches, they stop ranking and stop earning trust.

Already built and paid for: the hub pages, both interactive tools, the CMS and review workflow, and six drafted articles.
